# The corridor: the northern Øresund, October 1943

Research notes for *The Other Shore*. This file holds facts only, no game content.

**Recommendation.** Use the corridor proposed: departures from Helsingør and the coast just south of it (Snekkersten, Espergærde, Humlebæk, with Sletten and Nivå at the southern end), landing at Helsingborg and Råå. The sources show that this coast was heavily used in October 1943. Snekkersten alone sent about 1,000 people [S10][S11], local groups around Nivå helped about 900 [S7], and the Helsingør Sewing Club carried more than 700 Jews over the winter [S3]. Of the 197 people arrested while fleeing in the days after the action, about half were caught through a single Gestapo chief in Helsingør [S5]. The corridor also has the best-documented local organization, with a surviving boat held by USHMM [S3].

Two caveats:
- The departure points in the southern part of the corridor (Humlebæk, Sletten, Nivå) also sent boats toward Ven and Landskrona, not only toward Helsingborg [S6][S7]. The game's landing zone can therefore include Råå and the coast south of it.
- For records of arrival in Sweden, the Gilleleje–Höganäs route is better documented, because the Höganäs police archive survives in full while Helsingborg's is incomplete [S20]. That alternative is summarized in section 10.

**Tags.** **[C]** = documented for this corridor (Helsingør to Nivå, Helsingborg to Landskrona). **[G]** = general to the whole rescue, or documented elsewhere on the Sound. Where sources disagree, the range is given.

---

## 1. Was the corridor heavily used?

- [C] A source map shows departure points all along this coast: Hellebæk, Ålsgårde, Hornbæk and Dronningmølle to the north-west, and Helsingør, Snekkersten, Espergærde, Humlebæk, Sletten and Nivå to the south. Its arrows run from them to Helsingborg, Råå, Ven and Landskrona. (The map is from Kreth & Mogensen, *Flugten til Sverige*, p. 48, reproduced by folkedrab.dk.) [S6]
- [C] About 1,000 of the Jews who fled left through Snekkersten [S10][S11].
- [C] Kiær's escape operations left mainly from Snekkersten and Gilleleje [S4].
- [C] Through October 1943, Humlebæk was one of the largest gathering places on the east coast of North Zealand [S7].
- [C] About 900 Danish Jews escaped with the help of local groups in and around Nivå [S7].
- [C] The Helsingør Sewing Club moved about 1,400 people between October 1943 and May 1944, more than 700 of them Jews. Most of the Jewish refugees crossed in October 1943. [S3]
  - Sources disagree on the scale. A teaching handout says the club saved "thousands" [S9]. A Helsingborg teaching text credits Kiær with 1,400 refugees and about 300 resistance men carried back to Denmark [S8].
- [C] In the days after the action, 197 people in all were arrested while fleeing. About half of those arrests were due to one zealous Gestapo chief in Helsingør, Hans Juhl. This suggests how much flight activity there was in his area. [S5]
- [G] By Swedish coastal section, the same map draws its largest arrival pie chart for the central section (roughly Råå to Malmö, including Landskrona). The smaller chart is for the northern section (Helsingborg to Kullen). The thickest arrows run from Copenhagen to Landskrona and Malmö, and from Gilleleje to Höganäs and Kullen. This reading of the chart is approximate. [S6]

## 2. Geography

### 2a. Water and distances
- [C] The Øresund is 4 km wide at its narrowest point, between Helsingør and Helsingborg, and 118 km long, from the Gilleleje–Kullen line to the Stevns–Falsterbo line [S17].
  - Other sources for the crossing distance: "two and one-half miles" for Helsingør [S9]; "about three miles" for Helsingør–Helsingborg [S3]; a "four-mile" trip from Snekkersten [S1]; "roughly four miles" [S11].
- [C] The Nivå coast faces the island of Ven, and the folder calls it well suited to secret embarkation. There the Sound is narrow, and the trip by fishing boat took no more than three quarters of an hour. [S7]
- [G] On average a brackish surface current flows north, mainly along the Swedish coast. A saltier current flows south along the bottom. Strong wind can make the whole water column run one way. [S17]

### 2b. Danish departure coast (corridor)
- [C] **Helsingør.** The Sewing Club ran its route from here to Helsingborg [S3]. Couriers brought refugees to "a designated spot along the shore" to board [S3]. No specific beach in Helsingør is named in the sources read.
- [C] **Snekkersten.** A fishing village used as a departure point [S1][S13]. The Snekkersten Inn was the headquarters of a local group that the English sources call the "Friends of the Sound" [S10][S12].
- [C] **Espergærde.** Shown as a departure point on the source map [S6]. No narrative details were found.
- [C] **Humlebæk and Sletten.** Slettenhus (Hotel Gylfe) was a meeting place and hideout, and boats sailed from Sletten harbor [S16]. One source says Humlebæk harbor itself was under German control and too dangerous to use. Jetties and small piers along the coast were better suited. [S7]
- [C] **Nivå.** Nivaagaard brickworks had an old pier. Because the water there was shallow, refugees were rowed in keelless small boats out to a cutter anchored about 100 m offshore. [S7]

### 2c. Swedish landing coast
- [C] **Helsingborg.** The Kiær line ran from Helsingør to Helsingborg [S3]. Kiær and the boat stayed in Helsingborg during the day [S3].
  - One teaching text describes the most common route (in its simulation exercise, which mixes refugee and later resistance traffic) as Snekkersten to one of Helsingborg's quays [S8].
- [C] **A harbor near Helsingborg.** One family's oral-history summary records leaving from Snekkersten by fishing boat and landing at a Swedish harbor near Helsingborg [S13].
- [C] A wire report dated 1 October 1943 says about 300 Jews landed at Landskrona and Helsingborg in rowboats and other small craft [S23].
- [C] **Råå, Ven and Landskrona** appear as landing points on the source map [S6]. No narrative account of Råå landings in October 1943 was found (see section 11).
- [G] **North of Helsingborg.** Domsten, Viken, Höganäs and Kullen are shown on the map [S6]. Boats from Gilleleje landed at Nyhamn, Mölle, Lerhamn, Lerberget and Viken [S8].

### 2d. The boundary line in the water
- [G] Denmark and Sweden fixed the boundary of their territorial waters in the Sound by a declaration of 30 January 1932. In the northern Sound, down to the Lous Flak buoy, the line runs midway between the coast of Zealand and the Swedish mainland, with Ven disregarded. [S18]
  - Inference: at the 4 km narrows, the line lies roughly 2 km from each shore.
- [G] The Swedish minesweeper M 20 patrolled the Sound in 1941–45. Its log for October 1943 records taking Danish refugees aboard on many occasions, about a hundred people in all. The guidebook does not say where in the Sound this happened. [S19]
- [G] A Helsingborg teaching text reports two related actions [S8]:
  - It says that, on at least two occasions, the Swedish navy cut off German patrol boats that were hunting Kiær. No dates are given.
  - It says that one night in October 1943, in the southern Sound, a Swedish sea-patrol vessel maneuvered so that a German patrol boat turned away from a laden fishing boat.
- [C] The Sewing Club painted its boats flat gray to match the colour of Swedish coast guard vessels [S3].

## 3. Boats and the crossing

- [G] Most boats were fishing boats with crews from the coast [S5]. Large fishing boats and schooners were used, and so were small rowing boats and kayaks [S16].
- [C] The Sewing Club's first boat was borrowed, and it made only a few crossings before it was wrecked. A wealthy Jewish man from Helsingør then paid for a motorboat, *Lurifax*, which the club called "02". A third boat followed. Sympathetic port officials drew up false pilot's papers for Kiær. [S3]
- [C] *Lurifax* is a gray wooden motorboat, about 6.6 m long. It carried roughly 12 to 14 passengers. The catalog gives its speed in open water as 3 knots, although the same record also calls it "fast". [S3]
- [C] Kiær piloted it a couple of times per evening in good weather, with 12 to 14 passengers each time [S3]. The club ran as many as ten trips to Sweden a day [S9].
- [C] Kiær made about 142 round trips in all [S3][S8].
- [C] The Snekkersten crossings carried up to 12 people at a time [S1][S11].
- [C] Knud Parkow managed the Wiibroe brewery, which gave him the use of two freight vessels. He had a space built under the cargo crates that could hide 40 refugees. [S12]
- [C] The brewery's beer boat and pilot boats both carried mail and people [S8].
- [C] One large transport left Nivå on 8–9 October 1943 [S7]:
  - 230 Danish Jews were rowed out to a schooner and stayed below deck.
  - The schooner's engine would not start at midnight, and the ship lay off Nivå for about five hours.
  - At dawn a fishing boat came and towed it to Sweden.
- [G] Especially at the beginning, many crossings were made in rowing boats in complete darkness [S7]. Wire reports of the time describe rowboats crossing at night [S21][S23].
- [G] Boats from Gilleleje usually carried 20 to 30 refugees packed into the hold [S8].
- [G] Two named crossing lines on the Sound [S8]:
  - The "Kayak line", run by people from the Tre Torn rubber factory, used canoes and kayaks.
  - The "Stella line" was named after its fishing boat and run by a doctor and a ship chandler.
  - Both lines were uncovered after short periods, the Stella line by the Gestapo. The text does not say where they ran.
- [C] The regular Helsingør–Helsingborg ferry, though under German control, carried hundreds of people secretly, with the help of its crew [S8].
- [C] **Crossing times.** These range from about 45 minutes (Nivå, fishing boat) [S7] to about 50 minutes (Snekkersten, four miles) [S1].
  - [G] One source gives "a couple of hours" depending on route and weather [S16].
  - [G] Overloaded rowing boats in rough water could take an hour or more, and some capsized [S8].
- [G] **Below deck.** Hiding below deck or in the hold is documented [S7][S8][S12]. Hiding under nets or tarpaulins was not found in the sources read (see section 11).
- [C] **Sedatives (corridor).** Dr. Jørgen Gersfelt, a doctor in a fishing village near Helsingør (Snekkersten according to [S8]), agreed to use sedatives to keep children quiet during the crossing [S9]. He also gave calming medicine to adults who needed it [S8].
- [G] **Sedatives (general).** On some routes a doctor was attached who could sedate children during the crossing. Full sedation of small children was risky and did not always work, and some helpers and fishermen refused to take small children at all. [S7]
- [G] At least 150 Jewish children, often the youngest, stayed behind in Denmark with Danish guardians [S15][S5].
- [G] Stories that children were smothered or thrown overboard circulated as **rumors** [S7]. They are not documented events.

## 4. Organizers and helpers

- [C] **Helsingør Sewing Club (Helsingør Syklub).** Formed in October 1943 under a deliberately dull code name [S3]. Its four leaders were:
  - Erling Kiær (1903–1980), bookbinder and pilot;
  - Børge Rønne (1911–2001), journalist;
  - Thormod Larsen (1909–1998), police detective, who ran the club's network of police informants;
  - Ove Bruhn, police clerk, who handled communications and kept the accounts. [S3]
- [C] The club's route had other names: the "Kiær line", the "Police line" and the "H-H line" [S3].
- [C] Sources disagree on details of Kiær:
  - [S4] and [S3] describe him as a bookbinder with a business in Helsingør. [S8] calls him a book printer from Snekkersten.
  - Kiær registered as a refugee in Sweden in mid-October 1943, then kept making regular trips from Helsingborg to the Danish coast [S4].
- [C] A teaching handout counts five members by adding a doctor, Gersfelt [S9].
- [C] One Helsingborg text links the network to Helsingborg's police superintendent, Göte Friberg [S8].
- [C] **Snekkersten.** The innkeepers Henry Christian and Ellen Margrethe Thomsen belonged to the "Friends of the Sound". Their inn was the group's headquarters and sheltered Jews before the crossing. [S10]
  - A teaching text says the "Thomsen line" was run by the owner of Snekkersten Inn [S8].
- [C] **Couriers.** Preben Munch-Nielsen, a high-school student from Snekkersten, collected Jews at a nearby train station and led them through the woods to the shore [S11]. He also hid refugees in houses near the shore [S14]. He helped transport about 1,400 refugees [S1].
- [C] **Private homes.** Karina and Knud Parkow sheltered Jews in their home before crossings [S12].
- [C] **Doctors.** Dr. Jørgen Gersfelt is described in section 3 [S8][S9].
- [C] **Police.**
  - Most Danish police refused to cooperate with the German authorities [S3].
  - The Sewing Club worked closely with local and harbor police. They warned the club when and where patrols were expected, and other officers stood watch while refugees boarded. [S3]
- [C] **Taxis.** In Helsingør, Rønne saw ten strangers get out of two taxicabs and hurry into a friend's garage; they were Jews hiding from the roundup [S9]. In Nivå, refugees arrived on foot, by train, in cars and in trucks [S7].
- [C] **Brickworks.** Workers at the Nivaagaard brickworks hid refugees in the lofts of the ring kiln, where about 500 were sheltered [S16]. Local volunteers brought food during waits that could last several days [S7].
- [G] Helpers on land were not paid. The fishermen were. [S15][S7]

## 5. Payments and costs

- [G] The average price in October 1943 was about 1,000 kr per person, roughly 20,000 kr in today's money [S5][S15].
  - Some families paid up to 50,000 kr for a whole family [S5].
  - Prices were highest at the start, when few fishermen would sail [S5].
- [G] Most fishermen charged agreed prices, which the resistance kept low. Some profiteered, and a few sailed for free. [S5]
  - One local folder says that fewer than one transport in three was free [S7].
- [C] The Nivå folder gives an average of 850 kr per person in September 1943. Prices rose sharply during and after the October action. [S7]
- [G] By November 1943 the price to ferry a resistance fighter had fallen to 100 kr [S15].
- [G] About half of the money for the flight came from private, spontaneous collections [S15].
- [C] The Sewing Club charged only its costs: fuel, boat maintenance, and Kiær's daytime lodging in Helsingborg. Wealthier passengers paid more to cover those who could not pay. [S3]
- [C] The Parkows raised 10,000 kr [S12].
- [C] Sources disagree on whether people without money could travel:
  - One handout says no one was left behind for inability to pay [S9].
  - The Nivå folder says that if you could not pay, you did not go [S7].
- [G] Swedish arrival records note the payment terms for each crossing [S2][S20].

## 6. Hazards: documented, specific, and limited

- [G] **German surveillance at sea.** In October 1943 the Germans did not police the Øresund. Their patrol boats handled naval tasks, mainly mine clearance. Not a single boat carrying Jewish refugees was caught by German police. [S5]
  - A JTA wire report of 11 October 1943 claims that four refugee ships had been sunk [S22]. This contradicts [S5] and should not be stated as fact.
- [G] **Who did the hunting.** A small German group in Copenhagen pursued the fleeing Jews and depended on Danish informers [S5]. The German military was largely passive during the flight [S5].
- [C] **Gestapo Helsingør.** Hans Juhl, Gestapo chief in Helsingør, accounted for about half of the 197 arrests of people caught fleeing in the days after the action [S5].
  - The same article says the Gilleleje raid of 6 October alone led to about 50 arrests [S5].
- [G] **Totals.** In October 1943, 472 people were arrested and deported to Theresienstadt. 197 of them were caught while trying to flee to Sweden. [S5]
  - [S1] gives 470 deported and about 500 seized.
- [G] **Danish law.** Illegal departure was a crime under Danish law. On 6 October the Danish coast police closed Gilleleje harbor because of the crowds. [S5]
- [C] **Fear and rumor.** Refugees heard rumors of German patrol boats, searchlights and air surveillance [S7]. Treat these as what people feared, not as documented patrol routines.
- [C] **Fishermen's risk.** A fisherman who was intercepted could lose his boat, his livelihood, his freedom or his life [S7].
- [G] **Sea and weather.** Some refugees tried to row across on their own and drowned when small boats capsized in rough water [S3]. One teaching text recounts capsizes of overloaded rowing boats off North Zealand, without naming the harbor [S8].
- [C] **Weather.** October was wet, windy and cold [S7]. M 20's crew remembered "stormy" autumn weeks [S19]. No day-by-day record was found.
- [C] **Later incidents (after October).**
  - On 20 January 1944 a German patrol fired on a Sewing Club boat after a mix-up. Larsen was wounded; Rønne fled to Sweden; Bruhn went underground. [S3]
  - On 12 May 1944 a German patrol, probably tipped off by an informer, captured Kiær at sea. His boat was confiscated or sunk. [S3]
    - The capture place is given as off Ellekilde Hage [S4] or off Viken [S8].
  - After that, Kiær was held at Horserød, Vestre Prison and Frøslev, then deported to Neuengamme and Porta Westfalica. He was evacuated by the Swedish Red Cross. [S3]
  - Transports from Nivå continued through November, and from January 1944 they became harder as guarding increased [S7].
- [C] **Arrests of local helpers.**
  - Henry Thomsen was arrested in 1943 and released for lack of evidence. He was arrested again in August 1944 and died in Neuengamme four months later. [S10]
  - Gersfelt was later forced into hiding [S9].
  - Munch-Nielsen fled to Sweden in November 1943 [S11].
- [G] **Informers.** On 9 October in Tårbæk, after a crowd gathered at the harbor, an informer telephoned the Gestapo. A young man was shot and killed: a refugee hit by a warning shot according to [S5], a young Dane pushing off a boat according to [S15]. Thirteen people were arrested. (Tårbæk is south of this corridor.) [S5][S15]

## 7. Waiting, signals and judging conditions

- [C] **Getting to the shore.** Couriers met refugees at train stations and other agreed places and took them to selected hotels or neighbors' homes. There the refugees waited until the courier came back to guide them to the boarding spot. [S3]
  - The guides could move without lights during the blackout [S3].
- [C] **Where people waited.** Houses near the shore [S1][S14]; the Snekkersten Inn [S10]; Hotel Gylfe [S16]; the brickworks kiln lofts [S7][S16]; woods and holiday cottages [S16]. The Kronborg area was **not** found as a waiting place in any source read.
- [C] **Signals.** The Sewing Club used coloured signal lights in set patterns to mark safety, danger and landing places along the dark Danish coast [S3]. One text mentions helpers who flashed warning lights when danger approached [S8].
- [C] **Judging conditions.**
  - Kiær crossed in good weather [S3], although another text says his runs were so regular, whatever the weather, that people could set a clock by them [S8].
  - In Nivå, a waiting group moved from the lofts to the pier only when a fisherman was ready, and in silence [S7].
  - No source read explains how skippers judged wind or patrols. Only police warnings about patrols are documented [S3].

## 8. The rhythm of departures, late September to mid-October

- [G] **29 September.** The flight began in late September, once rumors spread [S5][S15]. The Swedish asylum offer was delivered on 29 September and broadcast on Swedish radio on 2 October [S5].
- [G] **28 September–3 October.** Swedish navy reports count at least 871 refugees reaching Sweden in these days [S5].
- [G] **1–2 October.** The German action began on the night of 1–2 October [S5].
  - [C] On 1 October, about 300 people landed at Landskrona and Helsingborg (wire report) [S23].
- [G] **By 7 October.** About 3,000 had reached Sweden [S21].
- [G] **Peak.** The source map divides arrivals into 27 Sep–3 Oct, 4–10 Oct and 11–17 Oct. In each coastal section the 4–10 October segment looks largest (reading of the chart) [S6]. At the height of the flight, transports carried hundreds of refugees across each night [S5].
- [G] **6–7 October.** Gilleleje harbor was closed (6 October), and the Gestapo raided the church loft that night [S5].
- [C] **8–9 October.** The large schooner transport left Nivå [S7]. [G] The same night, 124 refugees arrived at Höganäs [S8].
- [C] **Mid-October.** Kiær registered in Sweden but kept crossing [S4].
- [C] **November.** Transports continued [S7].
- [G] **Total.** 7,056 Jews fled to Sweden in October 1943, along with 686 non-Jewish spouses [S5]. [S1] gives about 7,200 Jews and 680 non-Jewish relatives.

## 9. Arrival on the Swedish side (the landing moment)

- [G] **Registration.** Refugees were registered by the local police district (landsfiskal district) where they landed; Skåne had about 100 such districts. The Social Board (Socialstyrelsen) in Stockholm then decided where they would stay, unless Swedish contacts took them in. [S20]
- [G] **The records.** They give route, time of arrival and payment terms [S2]. They are incomplete, but over 6,000 reports survive [S2].
  - In Höganäs the files give name, date of birth, occupation and payment, often with a photograph [S20].
- [C] **Helsingborg's records.** The Helsingborg local archive is incomplete [S20].
- [C] **Helsingborg's organizers.** Göte Friberg, the city's police superintendent, was central to organizing Helsingborg's reception of refugees [S8].
- [C] **Moving inland.** Relief work was centered in Helsingborg at first. On about 7 October, welfare officials decided to move Danish Jews away from the coastal districts. [S21]
  - Buildings at the Ramlösa spa and Örenäs castle, north of Landskrona, were given over to refugees [S21].
  - Representatives of Stockholm's Jewish community brought relief funds to Helsingborg, Landskrona and Malmö [S21].
- [G] **At the landing (Höganäs, documented example).** In Höganäs, customs officer Gunnar Nilsson handled registration [S20]. A Gunnar Nilsson from Nyhamn, probably the same man, met boats from Gilleleje and gave people warm blankets and hot drinks [S8]. Town doctor H.G. Widding and local residents examined all 124 arrivals of 8–9 October and found them lodging; Widding took families into his own home [S8].
- [G] **People's condition.** Refugees arrived cold, damp, frightened and seasick [S8], with few belongings [S22]. Children waited for their parents in Swedish fishermen's homes [S22].

## 10. Alternative corridors (for comparison)

- [G] **Gilleleje → Höganäs, Mölle, Viken.**
  - About 1,300 people of Jewish origin were helped to Sweden from Gilleleje [S24].
  - The Höganäs police archive holds detailed files on about 1,000 Jewish refugees who came by this route. It is the richest surviving Swedish local archive. [S20]
  - The best-known incident took place here: the church-loft arrests of 6–7 October [S5]. Sources disagree on the number arrested; see section 11.
  - Gilleleje fishermen later had to flee themselves [S25].
- [G] **Copenhagen and Dragør → Malmö, Limhamn, Landskrona.**
  - The source map shows the thickest arrows here [S6].
  - About 700 Jews left from Dragør until a German raid on 4 October closed it. The surviving cutter *Elisabeth K571* carried 70 of them. [S26]
- Verdict: the northern corridor is the best fit for one coherent setting with named local networks. The Gilleleje corridor is better documented on the Swedish (Höganäs) side.

## 11. Unverified: do not state as fact

- **Prices on this corridor in October 1943.** Only national averages, the Nivå folder's September figure, and the Sewing Club's cost-only policy are documented.
- **German patrol routines.** Exact routes, times, searchlights and aircraft over the northern Sound are known only as rumors [S7].
- **Kronborg.** Not found as a waiting or embarkation area.
- **Nets and tarpaulins.** Hiding passengers under them was not found in the sources read.
- **Råå.** No narrative of Råå landings in October 1943 was found; it appears only on the map.
- **Espergærde.** No details beyond the map.
- **The boundary line.** Swedish boats meeting Danish boats at the line in the Helsingør–Helsingborg narrows specifically in October 1943 is not documented in the sources read.
- **Villa Wisborg.** That it was the Gestapo headquarters in Helsingør comes from Wikipedia only.
- **The Gilleleje church loft.** Reported counts include more than 60 [S16], about 50 and over 100 within the same article [S5], and 80 or 85 in Wikipedia.
- **Sunk refugee ships.** The JTA claim of four sunk ships [S22] is contradicted by [S5].
- **Niels Bohr.** The anecdote in [S8] placing him in a 24-person fishing boat conflicts with other accounts. Do not use it.
- **Thomsen's own boat.** That he bought a boat himself appears only in search snippets of a Yad Vashem page that could not be fetched.
- **Gersfelt's other roles.** The use of friends' summer houses and his reworking of passenger lists so that the rich paid for the poor also come only from snippets of a Yad Vashem page that could not be fetched.
- **"Friends of the Sound".** The group's Danish name is unknown.
- **Weather and moon.** Day-by-day weather and moon phases for 1–17 October 1943 were not found.
- **Lurifax's speed.** The "3 knots" figure in [S3] is not reliable; the same record also calls the boat fast.

## 12. Safe gameplay abstractions

These are consistent with the record if they are presented as fiction.
- **Places.** A fictional cove, beach or small jetty between Snekkersten and Espergærde, or near Sletten. Unnamed beaches and piers were used, and couriers chose "designated spots" [S3][S7].
- **A fictional inn or boarding house** near the shore where refugees wait, rather than the real Snekkersten Inn or Hotel Gylfe [S3][S10][S16].
- **A fictional boat name.** Avoid documented names: *Lurifax*, *Solskin*/*Sunshine*, *Filuren*, *Elisabeth K571*, *Stella*, *Haabet*, *Britta*/*Svea*, *Marie* [S3][S9][S20][S25][S26], and also *Gerda III*, which is known only from Wikipedia.
  - The guide's *Ellen* collides with none of these. Note, though, that Ellen Margrethe Thomsen was a real Snekkersten rescuer [S10], so do not tie the boat to her.
- **Fictional fishermen, couriers and a fictional local doctor.** A doctor who calms children is documented in general [S7][S9]. Don't put invented actions on Kiær, Gersfelt, Thomsen, Juhl or Friberg.
- **Capacity by boat type.**
  - Rowing boat: two rowers and about nine passengers [S8].
  - Motorboat: 12–14 [S3].
  - Cutter: 20–30 in the hold [S8].
  - Freight boat: up to 40 hidden [S12].
  - A rare schooner: up to 230 [S7].
- **Crossing time.** About 45–60 minutes by motorboat or cutter in the narrows [S1][S7], longer by oar.
- **Waiting time.** Hours to several days [S7].
- **Prices.** Within the documented range (about 850–1,000 kr a head as an average, more early, sometimes nothing) [S5][S7]. Show them as one skipper's price, not a rule.
- **Signals.** Invented colours and patterns: coloured lights are documented, the codes are not [S3].
- **Weather.** Wet, cold, windy nights, seasickness, and a postponed or rerouted crossing [S7][S8].
- **Danger.** Danger should come from land: a noisy crowd, an informer, the Helsingør Gestapo. It should not come from sea chases, because German police caught no refugee boat in October 1943 [S5]. Police warnings about patrols are documented [S3].
- **Landing.** A changed landing place: Helsingborg, Råå or farther south [S6].
- **Arrival.** Registration by Swedish police or customs, a doctor's look, blankets and hot drinks, and children placed in fishermen's homes [S8][S20][S22].
