# Sweden and Finland: research notes

These notes support the Helsingborg reception campaign of *The Other Shore: October 1943* and the game's historical notes on Finland. They are research only. Every bullet cites a page I fetched and read; the sources list is at the end. Where sources disagree I give both. "Helsingborg" marks facts documented for that city; everything else is Skåne-wide or Sweden-wide and must not be presented as specific to Helsingborg. Documents of the time spell the city "Hälsingborg" [S2][S5].

## Part A: Sweden's reception, October 1943

### 1. The asylum announcement

- On 2 October 1943 a statement was read on Swedish radio. It said the Swedish minister in Berlin had, on 1 October, warned the Germans of the serious repercussions the measures against Danish Jews would have in Sweden, and had offered on behalf of the Swedish government to receive all Danish Jews. Anna Svenson quotes the text from the TT radio news archive (Riksarkivet), via Kirchhoff 1997, and dates the reading to 19.00 [S8].
- The Forum för levande historia timeline dates it 2 October 1943 and says Foreign Minister Christian Günther announced on the radio that all Jews from Denmark were welcome in Sweden [S10]. The Swedish Holocaust museum also attributes the radio announcement to the foreign minister [S11]. Sources differ on who read it and at what hour; the date, 2 October, is consistent.
- Contemporary reporting: on 4 October 1943 JTA reported Sweden's offer to receive "all 6,000 Jews from Denmark", backed by every Stockholm newspaper [S14]. On 8 October JTA reported that Chief Rabbi Ehrenpreis had visited the foreign minister to thank the government for the asylum [S16]. On 15 October JTA, citing the Danish Press Service, reported a Swedish démarche in Berlin about the Danish Jews and a protest against it by the Swedish National Socialist Party [S18].
- Niels Bohr: Wikipedia, citing Bohr's own account (Collected Works, 2005), Rhodes, Pais and others, says Bohr was taken to Sweden on 29 September 1943, refused to go on to the United States until Sweden publicly opened its doors to Danish Jews, and on 30 September persuaded King Gustaf V to make Sweden's willingness public [S9].
- Historians disagree about how decisive Bohr was. Rhodes and others see his appeal as a necessary precursor. Paul A. Levine does not mention Bohr and attributes the decision to Foreign Ministry instructions following a policy set in 1942. Levine also notes that on 31 August 1943 Gösta Engzell cleared the Swedish envoy in Copenhagen to issue Swedish passports to rescue Danish Jews [S9].
- Bohr attended the unveiling of Helsingborg's refugee monument on 21 May 1945 (Helsingborg) [S24].

### 1b. Earlier Swedish policy (brief)

- From 1938 Swedish policy was restrictive toward Jewish refugees. Sweden accepted the German "J" stamp, and in October 1938 a secret instruction told border posts to turn away holders of J-stamped passports who lacked entry documents [S11][S10]. In September 1938 a rule allowed foreigners presumed not to intend to return home to be refused entry [S8].
- On the Norwegian border, many Norwegians and German deserters were turned away in 1940–42 [S12].
- On the night of 25–26 November 1942 the remaining Jews of Oslo were arrested and deported on the *Donau*. About 770 Jews were deported from Norway in 1940–45 and about 34 survived; about 1,000 escaped to Sweden [S13].
- Swedish papers reported the Norwegian deportation on 27 November 1942, and protest meetings followed. On 3 December 1942 Prime Minister Per Albin Hansson cabled that Sweden was interested in receiving the Jews still in Norway [S10]. The Swedish Holocaust museum calls Norway the turning point, after which Sweden gradually opened its borders [S11].

### 2. Reception: who did what

**2a. Documented for Helsingborg**

- In October 1943 hundreds of refugees arrived every night. A camp opened at Ramlösa hälsobrunn (the spa) on 4 October, with major work by the Red Cross and the Lottakåren (women's voluntary defence corps). The Berga barracks and "other public spaces" were also used for a time [S20].
- Ramlösa served as refugee housing in autumn and winter 1943–44 [S26][S55]. JTA reported on 7 October that all buildings of the Ramlösa resort, and Örenäs castle north of Landskrona, had been given over to refugees [S15].
- Police: Göte Friberg joined the Helsingborg police in 1941. As polisintendent he was very active in refugee aid, with contacts in the Danish resistance [S23]. The city's study material calls him decisive in organising the city's reception [S29]. Friberg, police chief Ture K:son Bågenholm and Mayor Joël Laurin published an appeal for money for refugees in *Helsingborgs Dagblad* on 1 September 1943 [S25].
- Local authority: Laurin was involved in the reception. Later in September 1943 Föreningen Norden set up "Föreningen Nordens danska flyktinghjälp", and Laurin chaired its Helsingborg subcommittee [S25]. After 29 August 1943 the first Danish arrivals in Helsingborg were mainly soldiers and police [S25].
- The police "P.M." interrogation protocol was used by the Helsingborg police. It has a printed box "Jude" (Jew), answered yes or no [S5]. Some residence papers were signed by Hälsingborgs Poliskammaren [S5].
- The monument committee chose a police officer and a Lotta as the figures receiving the boat, because Lottas and police officers were often the first to receive Danish refugees; the police officer also stands for the aliens control [S24].
- Red Cross: the Helsingborg corps did its busiest work in 1940–50, including refugee work, quarantine stations and sanitation (delousing) facilities. In all it helped 19,995 refugees, internees, invalids and children of 31 nations during and right after the war [S21]. This figure covers all those years, not only October 1943.
- Den danske Club was formed by Danish refugees at a meeting in Konsumhuset in August 1943, with premises on Bollbrogatan. It was a contact point with the authorities, ran teaching and a restaurant at cost price, offered Danish newspapers, and closed on 10 June 1945 [S22]. It had almost 5,000 members by the end of the war [S29].
- 4,612 refugees from Denmark settled in Helsingborg during the war [S22]. About 20,000 refugees passed through Helsingborg in 1940–45 [S20][S28].
- After a medical examination, interrogation and care, refugees were sent on to larger camps inland or to private homes. Many stayed and worked in Helsingborg's large industries [S20].
- Jewish community: on 7 October 1943 JTA reported that Stockholm Jewish community representatives had gone to Malmö, Landskrona and Helsingborg with "extensive relief funds" to help local relief committees and authorities. The same report said aid work had so far been centralised at Helsingborg [S15]. Helsingborg has a synagogue today [S38]. I found no source on what the Helsingborg Jewish community itself did in 1943.
- Civil defence, the home guard, the Red Cross, the Lottakåren and the police received the White Buses arrivals in April 1945 and housed them in schools and public buildings [S20]. This is documented for 1945, not 1943.

**2b. Elsewhere in Skåne**

- Höganäs: the town physician H.G. Widding and residents worked through the night of 8–9 October, when 124 refugees landed, to examine everyone and find lodgings (city study material, no footnotes) [S29].
- Fishing harbours north of Höganäs (Nyhamn, Mölle, Lerhamn, Lerberget, Viken) received boats from Gilleleje, often carrying 20–30 people in the hold. Locals met them with blankets and hot drinks [S29].
- Landing places recorded in the Swedish protocols include Hälsingborg, Råå, Domsten, Hittarp, Viken, Höganäs, Mölle, Arild, Landskrona, Barsebäck, Lomma, Malmö/Limhamn, Klagshamn, Skanör/Falsterbo, Trelleborg, Ystad, Simrishamn and Ven [S2].
- The study material claims most of the 7,000 refugees landed between Helsingborg and Höganäs [S29]. The port list above shows a much wider spread, so do not state this as fact.
- Malmö: the Jewish community had run a relief committee since 1938, and in autumn 1943 it took part in receiving many Danish Jews [S34].
- Lund: about 450 Danish refugees were there at the peak. A university refugee bureau, chaired by the rector, was formed on 7 October 1943 [S8].
- Navy: the Föreningen M 20 and the Sjöhistoriska museet describe the navy's work to save thousands of Danish refugees in autumn 1943; the minesweeper M 20 protected and swept Swedish waters and rescued refugees [S37]. The study material says the navy twice cut off German patrol boats chasing the Kjær boat [S29].

**2c. Sweden-wide**

- Stockholm Jewish community: on 12 October 1943 it appealed for help for Denmark's Jews, noting that most had arrived destitute [S10]. The JDC sent an initial $25,000 to the Mosaiska Församlingen, which it called Sweden's leading Jewish welfare organisation [S17].
- Refugees could settle where they found work or a place in a camp (förläggning). Living in Malmö, Göteborg or Stockholm required permission from the Socialstyrelsen (National Board of Social Welfare) [S8].
- Orthodox Jews were given separate housing; the best known was the Helsjön folk high school in Västergötland [S8].
- On 7 October 1943 the welfare authorities decided to move Danish Jewish refugees away from the coastal districts [S15].

### 3. Procedures

- First contact: every refugee received a Nödfallsvisering (emergency visa) at the first meeting with Swedish authorities; few survive in the files [S5].
- Interrogation: the "Uppgift" interrogation protocol was taken on arrival. The ideal form records name, date and town of birth, nationality, religion, title, address, education, marital status, children, reason for flight, time and place of departure, time and place of arrival, money brought, and date of report [S5].
- Other forms: Främlingspass (alien's passport) for those without passport or ID; Landsfiskalrapport (rural police chief); police reports from city criminal departments to the Socialstyrelsen; reports to county administrations (länsstyrelser); and visa applications (Ansökan om visering) to the Socialstyrelsen's aliens bureau, filed after the emergency visa and the interrogation [S5].
- The authorities recorded name, birthplace, residence, occupation, spouse and children, and used this to judge which trades refugees could contribute and how much state support was needed [S4].
- Most children under 16 who came with their parents had no report of their own and appear in the parents' report. A few unaccompanied children had their own [S4][S3].
- Payments to boatmen: the protocols shed light on routes, arrival times and "payment conditions" [S1][S3]. Wikipedia, citing folkedrab.dk, says fishermen typically charged about 1,000 kroner a person, and some far more [S9].
- Medical examination is documented for Helsingborg [S20] and Höganäs [S29]. The Helsingborg Red Cross worked at quarantine stations and sanitation (delousing) facilities at some point in 1940–50 [S21], and White Buses arrivals in April 1945 went first to a sanitation station (sanitetsanstalt) at Rönnowska skolan [S29]. Neither is documented for Danish arrivals in October 1943 (see Unverified).
- Clothing and money: the Danish legation's refugee office supplied warm clothing, for example to people lodged in summer spa hotels, and paid doctors' and dentists' bills, schooling and deposits. Standard rates were 175–200 kr a month for a single person unable to work, 325–350 kr for a couple, and 45–50 kr more per child. Recipients had to have no more than 150 kr in assets and to support themselves by work where possible [S30].
- Accommodation: hostels, hotels, youth hostels and summer spa hotels [S30]; camps and private homes [S20]; in Lund, pupils without family there lived with friends, lodged in private homes, or stayed in the school's boarding houses in town [S8].
- Onward travel: refugees were sent from Helsingborg to larger camps inland or to private homes [S20]. I found no source that names rail as the means.
- Records and lists: the refugee office built a refugee card index [S30]. The Danish Refugee Administration's "Central Register of Refugees, 1943–1945" (49 parcels) and the legation refugee office's "Name Index of refugees, 1943–1945" are in the Danish National Archives (Rigsarkivet) [S35][S36].
- The Helsingborg study material tells of small children who stood for a long time on refugee lists as "unknown" and were later found by relatives [S29]. The source is uncited; treat it as illustrative, not as a documented case.
- Den danske Club in Helsingborg issued school questionnaires for Danish children, sent to the Socialstyrelsen [S5].

### 4. Organisations, work, school, numbers

- The refugee office (Flygtningekontoret) was set up by the Danish legation in Stockholm after 29 August 1943. It was staffed by experienced civil servants and by Danes of Jewish and non-Jewish background, often resistance members. It administered aid, kept the card index and planned the return home. Sweden paid most of the aid; the Danish debt of about 50 million kr was waived after the war. It closed in June 1945 (30 June in the article's body text) [S30].
- The Danish Refugee Administration's general department handled refugees who were out of work and not in barracks: clothing, lodging, upkeep, help for the elderly, mothers and pregnant women, and medical and dental aid [S35].
- Work: on 18 October 1943 JTA reported that refugees from Denmark were exempt from the three-year bar on aliens starting businesses, and could get permits to work for Swedish firms [S19]. Some passport requests in the files are on Husqvarna arms factory letterhead [S5].
- Schools: the Danish school in Lund was started by the refugee committee with the legation's refugee office, which granted 10,000 kronor on 7 November 1943. It graduated 32 students and 25 lower-secondary pupils on 1 July 1944, had between 278 and 390 pupils over time (another source says 384), and officially closed on 9 June 1945. Danish schools also existed in Göteborg, Helsingborg and Norrköping [S8].
- Danish Brigade: formed on 15 November 1943 at Sofielund in Älmeboda under Major General Kristian Knudtzon as "police troops", approved by Sweden in December 1943. By May 1945 it had almost 5,000 soldiers and women auxiliaries. It was dissolved on 10 July 1945 [S31]. About 750 of its members were Jews [S32]. On where it sailed from on 5 May 1945, lex.dk says Malmö [S31] and the Helsingborg lexicon says Helsingborg (5–6 May) [S27]; both may be right.
- Numbers in October 1943: about 7,200 Jews and 700 non-Jewish relatives (USHMM) [S6][S7]; 7,500 Jews and 686 non-Jewish spouses (Goldberger, via Wikipedia) [S9]; about 6,000 Jews and 2,000 others (Helsingborg study material) [S29]. JTA estimated 3,000 had arrived by 7 October [S15].
- Totals: about 7,000 Jews and about 11,000 non-Jewish Danes, mostly from the resistance or with a military background; 119 Danes had fled before 29 August 1943 [S30]. The Danish Jewish Museum gives about 18,000 in all by 1945 [S33]. About 3,300 concentration camp prisoners came on the White Buses in April 1945 [S30]. At the end of 1944 Sweden held 180,000 refugees of all nationalities [S8].

### 5. The Danish Jewish Museum's "Safe Haven" material

- In 2018 the museum and the Danish Institute for International Studies (DIIS) published the Swedish refugee protocols, copies from Riksarkivet in Stockholm, for the 75th anniversary [S1][S3].
- The database holds over 6,000 reports of about 20 report types. It is not complete, and more material is in Swedish archives [S1][S3].
- The originals are in the archive of the Statens utlänningskommission's office at Riksarkivet, in series F 1 ABA, F 1 ABB, F 1 ABC, F 1 AC and F 1 B [S4].
- The most common types are Uppgift, P.M., Ansökan om visering, Främlingspass and an unidentified type [S5]. Field contents are summarised in section 3.
- Most refugees were Jews by religion or by Nazi racial definition, plus a large share of non-Jews, mainly spouses in mixed marriages [S3].
- Some people are missing because Swedish authorities often discarded temporary residence papers when a person moved on to another country [S3].
- The database leaves out reports on people born in 1930 or later, people who arrived before 1943 or after 1945, people clearly named as resistance members, reports with incriminating data, and former Theresienstadt prisoners who came on the White Buses [S4].
- The search form uses departure port, arrival port, birth year and whether children were present [S2].

### 6. Return to Denmark, May–June 1945

- Planning began before liberation on 5 May 1945. Returnees gathered at Copenhagen Central Station and received travel money there [S30].
- The refugee office published a booklet, *Raad og Anvisninger vedrørende Hjemrejsen* (advice on the journey home). According to the museum's teaching sheet, it was tasked with organising the return in October 1944 [S33].
- Helsingborg: according to Friberg's memoir, quoted in the lexicon, refugees in the Helsingborg sector were the first mass transport home, on 28 May 1945, in two full ferries. An exit ban was still in force and helped keep the flow orderly [S22]. Den danske Club closed on 10 June 1945 [S22].
- Lund: the school officially closed on 9 June 1945; a school memoir has the board chairman, Aage Bertelsen, throwing the school keys overboard as the group sailed home from Malmö [S8].

## Part B: Finland (short)

### 7. Finland's own Jewish citizens

- "As Finnish citizens, the Jews fought alongside others"; Finland "did not, however, hand over its Jewish citizens" [S39].
- More than 300 Finnish Jewish soldiers and Lotta auxiliaries served in the Continuation War. Twenty-three Jews fell in the wars, and five are still missing [S40]. Wikipedia gives 327 soldiers, 21 in the Lotta Svärd, and 15 killed in the Winter War and 8 in the Continuation War [S42].
- The Fenno Judaica exhibition calls Finland the only country fighting on Germany's side that did not restrict Jews' civil liberties [S40].

### 8. The deportation of eight refugees, 6 November 1942

- About 500 Jewish refugees reached Finland before the war, and 350 moved on. In 1942 the rest were moved to the countryside (Lammi, Hauho), and able-bodied men were sent to forced labour in Salla in late winter 1942 [S41].
- The Ministry of the Interior and the State Police (Valpo) decided, "on fabricated grounds", to hand over eight Jewish refugees who were not Finnish citizens [S40]. HAM calls them stateless [S43]; Wikipedia calls them Austrian [S42]. Their origins are not settled in the sources I read.
- On 2 November 1942 Jonas Jakobson, chairman of the Helsinki Jewish community, appealed to the Council of State [S41]. The deportation went ahead on 6 November 1942 [S41][S44]. Wikipedia says 19 other people were deported with them, 27 in all [S42].
- They were handed to the Gestapo in Helsinki's South Harbour [S44] and taken on the s/s *Hohenhörn* to Tallinn, then to Auschwitz [S43]. They reached Auschwitz early in 1943 [S44].
- Names as listed in a 2000 report: Heinrich and Kurt Hupper, Georg, Jan and Franz Olof Kollman, Hans Martin Korn, Elias Kopelowsky and Hans Szybilskij [S44]. Spellings vary between sources (for example Huppert, Szubilski) [S42]. The group included a young family and a father with his ten-year-old son [S43].
- Outcome: seven died and one survived. Only one of the eight survived [S41] [S44]; the survivor was Georg Kollmann, who lived through Auschwitz and the labour camps [S41], having lost his wife and child at Auschwitz [S40].
- Apology: Prime Minister Paavo Lipponen apologised to the Jewish community, on behalf of the government and all Finns, when he unveiled the memorial on Tähtitorninmäki in Helsinki [S44]. Fenno Judaica dates this 6 November 2000 [S40]. Reports of 5–6 November 2000 place it on 5 November [S44][S45][S46]. JTA said it marked the 58th anniversary [S47]. Use "November 2000" unless the date is resolved. The memorial is by Rafael Wardi and Niels Haukeland [S43] [S44].
- The Globe and Mail dates the 1942 handover to 6 December [S45]; every other source says 6 November.
- In May 1944 the remaining refugees received visas and moved to Sweden [S41].

### 9. Other handovers (brief, secondary sources only)

- In 1942 Finland exchanged about 2,600–2,800 Soviet POWs for about 2,100–2,200 Finnic POWs held by Germany [S48][S42]. The count of Jews among them differs: 74 [S48] or 47 [S42]. Do not state a number.
- Citing *Helsingin Sanomat* (2008), Wikipedia says Valpo handed about 500 POWs and refugees to the Germans, about 10 percent of them Jews, and that their ethnicity seems not to have been the reason [S49].
- After Elina Sana's 2003 book, the National Archives began research under Jussi Nuorteva [S50]. Wikipedia cites its 2008 report (ed. Westerlund) [S48]. I could not read the report itself.

## Part C: Postwar collective-expulsion check

- Sweden had no postwar expulsion of an ethnic-German minority in the sources I read. The comparable postwar controversy was the 1945–46 extradition to the USSR of interned soldiers who had served in German forces: about 2,700 Germans, 150 Austrians and 150 Balts in all; 153 Balts and 227 Germans were put aboard the *Beloostrov* at Trelleborg on 25 January 1946 [S51]. Another source gives about 2,400 Germans and 146 Balts [S52]. This was the handover of military internees, not the collective punishment of a civilian minority.
- Finland: under the 1944 armistice, Finland had to intern German and Hungarian civilian nationals. 470 people were held from autumn 1944 to March 1946, including Finnish-born women and children. A 2014 law compensated them, the justice minister calling the internment "unreasonable" [S53]. Finland also handed 2,546 German POWs from the Lapland War to the USSR [S48].
- The categories differ: POWs, military internees and enemy-alien civilians, not a resident German minority. Do not claim that Denmark, Sweden and Finland uniformly retained all ethnic Germans, German citizens, refugees or soldiers; both Sweden and Finland handed over Germans [S51][S48].
- Flag for the Denmark researcher: in April 1940 Helsingborg police lodged about twenty anti-Nazi Germans arriving from Denmark in the old customs house, with bedding from the Red Cross [S29].

## Unverified / do not state as fact

- An exact hour for the 2 October broadcast. 19.00 rests on one source [S8]; a "6 a.m." broadcast appeared only in search snippets and was not read.
- Quarantine or delousing of Danish refugees in October 1943.
- Onward travel by train in 1943, and from which stations.
- Specific roles of customs, the civilian coast guard or civil defence in October 1943.
- What the Helsingborg Jewish community did in 1943.
- Which Helsingborg schools, if any, housed refugees in 1943. Pålstorp school was emergency housing for refugees "for a time", with no date given [S54].
- The study material's story that Bohr crossed in a 24-person fishing boat shielded by a Swedish patrol vessel [S29]. It conflicts with other accounts.
- How families split across landing points were actually reunited.
- The number of Jews among the Soviet POWs Finland handed over; the national origins of the eight deported in 1942; the exact date of the Lipponen apology.
- Wikipedia's claim that seven of the eight were murdered "immediately" [S42]. Other sources say they reached Auschwitz in early 1943.

## Safe gameplay abstractions

- A fictional school, church hall or other public building used as a reception centre. Helsingborg used the Ramlösa spa, the Berga barracks and "other public spaces" [S20]. Name the building fictionally, and do not claim a real school was used in 1943.
- Fictional Red Cross and Lottakåren volunteers, and a fictional police officer who takes Uppgift-style interviews using the real field list [S5][S20][S24].
- A fictional emergency-visa slip and interview form modelled on the Nödfallsvisering and Uppgift, including "money brought" [S5].
- Fictional host families and boarding houses, and fictional transfers inland [S20][S30].
- A fictional noticeboard or name list at a club modelled on Den danske Club, as long as it is not presented as a documented reunification system [S22].
- Fictional local appeals and collections, modelled on the 1 September 1943 appeal [S25].
- Keep real people (Friberg, Laurin, Bohr) to documented roles. Use no survivor testimony. Present children under 16 as recorded on their parents' forms [S4]. The "Jude" box on the P.M. form [S5] may appear, framed with care.

## Open questions

- Svenson's 19.00 radio statement versus Günther as speaker: was it one broadcast or several?
- Did Helsingborg have a dedicated landing control point at the harbour in October 1943, and who ran it?
- Did the Danish Brigade sail from Malmö, Helsingborg or both [S27][S31]?
- Was there a central Swedish list of refugees by landing point, or only the per-person protocols [S4]?
