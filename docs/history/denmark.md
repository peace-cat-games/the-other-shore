# Denmark, October 1943: the warning, the escape and its aftermath

Research notes for "The Other Shore: October 1943". Every bullet ends with its source tag [S#], and each tag is a page that was fetched and read (November 2026). Where sources disagree, the bullet says who gives which figure. The range is the safe claim; no single number is "the" figure. Danish quotations are short phrases from the source. There are no survivor quotations.

Read sections 3, 5, 6 and 8 before writing any number into the game. These are the places where sources differ most.

---

## 1. Context: the community and 29 August 1943

- When Germany occupied Denmark (9 April 1940) the Jewish population was about 7,500 (0.2% of the population). About 6,000 were Danish citizens; "the rest were German and eastern European refugees". [S1]
- Between 1933 and the occupation Denmark admitted about 4,500 German and eastern European Jewish refugees, about 3,000 of them *chalutzim* (young pioneers training for emigration to Palestine). The page does not say how many stayed. Its 1940 figures (7,500 in all, about 6,000 of them citizens) imply that many moved on, but that is an inference. [S1]
- "Most Jews lived in" Copenhagen [S1]. Kirchhoff gives about 5,000 of roughly 8,000 as living in Copenhagen [S22].
- Totals for 1943 vary: "about 6500" (folkedrab English pages) [S18]; about 7,500 (USHMM) [S4]; "ca. 8.000" (Kirchhoff, Rühl) [S22][S10]. Bak counts 8,214 people of Jewish background nationally who either fled to Sweden or were deported [S14]. Part of the spread depends on whether "half-Jews", non-Jewish spouses and refugees are counted.
- Among the roughly 8,000 who reached Sweden were German-Jewish refugees with residence permits, "about 2,000" (Bak 2009) [S8].
- The community was mixed: families in Denmark for centuries, descendants of Russian immigrants who came around 1900, and German Jews fleeing the Nazis (Danish Jewish Museum) [S9]. In 1943, 49% of Danish Jews belonged to the working class (Bak) [S12].
- Danish authorities also expelled Jewish refugees: 21 were sent back to Nazi Germany during the occupation [S17]. Bak gives 20 expelled [S8].
- 29 August 1943: the Danish government resigned rather than accept new German demands (trials of saboteurs in German military courts) [S1]. The Wehrmacht took executive power and interned the Danish army and navy. Police and courts kept working [S10]. The government's cooperation policy had protected the Jews, and that protection ended with it [S8][S22].
- 8 September 1943: Werner Best (Reich plenipotentiary) sent telegram No. 1032 proposing deportation while the state of emergency lasted [S23][S10]. Hitler approved on 17 September [S10][S23]. Ribbentrop approved and ordered it carried out on 25 September [S10].

## 2. The warning

**Duckwitz**
- Georg F. Duckwitz was the shipping expert (maritime attaché) at the German legation and a close associate of Best [S23][S8].
- He had been warning Jewish acquaintances since 17 September (his diary, per Kirchhoff) [S23]. On 13 September he flew to Berlin to try to stop the plan and failed. On 21–22 September he went to Stockholm and met Prime Minister Per Albin Hansson. Kirchhoff notes that there is no trace of that meeting in Swedish archives, and that it was not decisive for Swedish policy [S23].
- 28 September 1943: Best told Duckwitz the date of the action. Duckwitz passed it to his Danish and Swedish contacts, "including Hans Hedtoft and his friends" [S23]. Rühl names Hans Hedtoft and H. C. Hansen [S10]. USHMM says he "secretly informed the Danish resistance" [S2]. The Jewish Virtual Library (citing Yad Vashem) says "Danish Social-Democratic friends" [S25].
- The Social Democrats passed the warning to the leadership of Det Mosaiske Troessamfund (the Jewish community), which at first refused to believe it [S10].
- Yad Vashem recognized Duckwitz as Righteous Among the Nations on 29 March 1971 [S25].

**The synagogue warning**
- Rabbi Marcus Melchior tried to warn the community "under nytårsgudstjenesten et par dage før aktionen" (at the New Year service a few days before the action). The source is his son Bent Melchior's account [S10]. Bak also says Melchior was warned through an acquaintance of the Social Democrat Alsing Andersen [S11].
- TIME (2015): the warning came on the eve of Rosh Hashanah, which began on 29 September 1943. Melchior cancelled the service and urged people to "hide or flee" [S26].
- Calendar check: 29 September 1943 was Erev Rosh Hashana. The holiday began at sunset that Wednesday [S27].
- USHMM caption: Melchior "warned his congregants" and himself hid and escaped to Sweden [S6].
- Copenhagen's synagogue celebrated its centenary in 1933 with King Christian X present [S1].

**How word spread**
- Late September: "et rygte om en forestående massearrestation" (a rumour of an imminent mass arrest) spread through the Jewish community. Most families left home quickly [S11]. Bak (2009): the warning "spread like wildfire". Only those too old, sick or alone, or unwilling, stayed home [S8].
- The flight had begun in late September, "several days before the official warning" [S8].
- The helpers were loose networks built on personal trust: scouts, the Social Democratic youth (DSU), students and teachers at the University of Copenhagen, much of the capital's medical staff, and parish priests (Stræde) [S13]. Doctors, nurses, drivers and fishermen were especially prominent, and taxi drivers and hauliers were valued because they had vehicles (Bak) [S12].
- Police: "In general, the Danish police authorities refused to cooperate" [S1]. USHMM says the rescue grew to include "the police" [S2]. Bak (2009) records Danish police officers helping at Tårbæk harbour. She adds that coastguards sometimes helped but mostly cooperated with the German police, and that railway staff stayed passive [S8].
- Sweden: the Swedish envoy delivered an offer of asylum on 29 September. It was announced on Swedish radio on 2 October [S11].

## 3. The German action, night of 1–2 October 1943

- The action began Friday 1 October at about 21:00 [S10]. It was planned for an evening when Jews were expected to be home for Rosh Hashanah, and it had to be finished within 3 hours [S8][S11].
- **Forces (sources differ):** "1.500 tyske politisoldater" (Bak) [S11]; "more than 1,800 German policemen" (Bak 2009) [S8]; an Ordnungspolizei force of 1,500 plus "3000 mand fra det tyske sikkerhedspoliti" (Rühl) [S10]. Danish Nazis acted as guides [S10]. USHMM simply says "German police" [S1].
- Best had ordered that doors were not to be broken down [S10][S11]. Some families escaped arrest by not answering. Elsewhere police got in with keys from caretakers and neighbours [S11].
- **Arrests that night (sources differ):** German sources record "kun" (only) 232 Jews arrested that night, about 200 of them in the Copenhagen area (Rühl) [S10]. "Only 202 were arrested in Copenhagen" (Bak 2009, citing Kreth & Mogensen) [S8]. The ship *Wartheland* left Langelinie on 2 October with 198 Jewish and 150 communist prisoners for Swinemünde [S17]. Duckwitz's diary for 2 October mentions a ship with "200 (!!)" elderly Jews [S23]. A train left Aalborg on 2 October (stops at Randers and Kolding) and reached Theresienstadt on 5 October with 83 prisoners [S17]. In Odense, Jews were held at Vestre Skole on 2 October and taken to Kolding [S28].
- After the raid there were no further roundups. Arrests were left to a small group of Gestapo men [S8]. Bak gives 197 people caught while trying to flee [S11][S8], about half of them because of one Gestapo chief, Hans Juhl in Helsingør [S11][S8].

**German officials did not act alike (keep this nuance)**
- Kirchhoff argues that Best launched the "final solution" in Denmark with his 8 September telegram but then ran a "dual strategy". To Berlin he presented himself as a supporter; locally he tried to delay the action, soften it or even sabotage it. Duckwitz was a pawn in that game but also "an independent, dynamic force", and that "does not make Best the 'rescuer of the Danish Jews'" [S23]. In Lex, Kirchhoff writes that Best "saboterede ... selv aktionen" by leaking the timing through Duckwitz [S22].
- Bak (2009): Best had all the police he needed but "took every step to ensure that the raid would not produce results". He preferred to drive the Jews out rather than hunt them down [S8].
- Navy and coast: according to Kirchhoff, Duckwitz got the Copenhagen harbour commander, Cammann, to keep German patrol boats in harbour, a promise "easy to keep" because the navy lacked the boats [S23]. Bak: there was no German police surveillance of the Øresund in October 1943. Patrol boats did only naval work, mainly minesweeping, and none of the 600–700 transports was seized at sea [S8][S11].
- Wehrmacht: Bak says it "forholdt sig overvejende passivt" (was largely passive) despite orders to assist [S11]. Kirchhoff says it tried to stay out but was ordered to support the action [S23]. Bak 2009 says instead that it was "ordered not to interfere", and that eyewitnesses saw soldiers look the other way [S8]. **These accounts conflict on the order itself.** General von Hanneken had preferred to postpone the action [S10].
- Security police chief Rudolf Mildner and Paul Kanstein went to Berlin and tried to halt the action [S23].
- Not everyone held back: Hans Juhl, a "særligt nidkær" (especially zealous) Gestapo chief, made about half of the later arrests [S11].

## 4. Hiding before the crossing

- Families "sov i dagevis hos venner, naboer og fremmede" (slept for days with friends, neighbours and strangers who took them in) [S11]. USHMM: homes, hospitals and churches [S2]. Help ranged from lending "et sommerhus" (a summer house) to organizing departures from harbours and beaches [S12].
- Hospitals: "Flere hospitaler i hovedstaden" (several hospitals in the capital) served as collection points and hiding places. Refugees were registered under non-Jewish names and smuggled out in ambulances [S12].
- Bispebjerg Hospital: the hospital itself (in a 2016 letter to Copenhagen's street-naming committee) names surgeon Karl Henrik Køster (1909–1970) as "en af de drivende kræfter i flugtarbejdet". It says deputy matron Agnes Louise Korsholm's flat served for a time as a "flugtcentral" in October 1943, and that she raised money and recruited the resident nurses to help "flere tusinder jøder" [S34]. In 1963 doctors at Chicago's Michael Reese Hospital funded a fellowship at Bispebjerg in appreciation of its role in the rescue [S35].
- Gilleleje: refugees hid in the parish hall and the church loft (see §6) [S11].
- **How long:** no source gives an average. The timeline below implies most hid for a few days up to about two weeks:
  - at least 871 had crossed between 28 September and 3 October [S11];
  - more than 500 were in Skåne by 2 October [S10];
  - the peak came on 8–9 October, with up to 2,500 crossing in 48 hours [S10];
  - another 2,000 crossed in the second week [S10];
  - by mid-October nine out of ten were in Sweden [S10].
- USHMM puts the whole flight at "little more than three weeks" [S2], or "about a month" [S1].
- About 100 people of Jewish descent stayed in Denmark, hidden, for the rest of the war [S11]. Children left with non-Jewish foster families or in children's homes: "at least 150" (Bak, folkedrab; videnskab.dk) [S11][S28], or "approximately 130" (Bak 2009) [S8]. Some stayed until liberation, 22 months later [S8]. On some routes children under five were refused because of the noise risk [S28].

## 5. Money

**Prices per person (documented ranges only)**
- "The cost per person ranged between 500 and 2,000 kroner" (Bak 2009). For scale, an industrial worker earned about 2 kroner an hour [S8].
- The average for October 1943 was 1,000 kr. per person (Bak). Her rough multiplier is ×20 for present-day value [S11][S12][S28].
- Some families paid "op til 50.000 kr." for a family [S11][S12][S8]. Prices in "flugtens første dage" (the first days of the flight) "kunne nå op på svimlende beløb" (could reach dizzying sums) [S11].
- Journalism without a cited source: the Copenhagen Post (2018) says 3,000 kr. per person was "not uncommon" [S36]. That is above Bak's range, so use it with care.
- For comparison: a skilled worker's monthly wage was 414 kr. A new fishing boat cost 15,000–30,000 kr. [S12].

**Who paid**
- The flight cost an estimated "op imod 20 millioner kr." in total. Jewish families probably paid about half; the rest came from collections and donations [S12][S28].
- Large sums often paid for a whole boatload or for poor Jews who could not pay [S12]. The resistance collected significant amounts from firms and private people so that no one was left behind for lack of money [S12]. Wealthy individuals also paid (Stræde) [S13]. The Copenhagen Post says rescue groups arranged for wealthy Jews to pay more so that poorer ones could cross [S36].
- Jews raised money from their own assets, loans or wealthier Jews (Bak, in videnskab.dk). Historians consider it plausible that King Christian X donated [S28].
- Fishermen were the only part of the rescue that was systematically paid. Most other help on land was free [S12][S28].

**Why prices changed**
- Prices followed supply and demand. At first few fishermen were willing and demand was high. Later most sailed "for aftalte priser, der blev holdt nede af modstandsbevægelsen" (for agreed prices that the resistance kept down), and a few sailed free [S11].
- "Efter transporterne i oktober" (after the October transports) the price of an illegal crossing fell to about 100 kr. per person [S12]. Videnskab.dk dates the 100 kr. fare (for a resistance member) to November 1943 [S28]. This drop is documented for after October; no fetched source gives figures for a fall within October.

**Fishermen's and helpers' risk**
- Illegal departure was forbidden under Danish law [S11]. Helpers caught by the Gestapo were at first handed to the Danish police and courts, where the maximum was "tre måneders fængsel" (three months in prison) [S12][S8]. Many cases never reached court, or the accused were let go [S8][S12].
- The first known verdict, on 23 October 1943, acquitted the helpers. On appeal, after German pressure, they got three months [S12].
- Helpers who kept running routes after October risked arrest, prison and deportation [S12].
- Boats could be lost through accident or "beslaglæggelse" (seizure). Compensation after the war went only to those who had not profited [S12].
- Bak (2009): "Contrary to myth, the rescuers did not risk their lives to save the Jews." At least 46 people died as a direct result of the action: 16 suicides, 21 drowned, 6 from exhaustion or illness and 3 shot. Two of them were helpers, one drowned rowing refugees and one a suicide in a Gestapo cell [S8][S12].
- At Tårbæk on 9 October a young refugee was killed by a warning shot. Thirteen people were arrested and five later deported [S11][S8].

## 6. Gilleleje

- Gilleleje was one of the main departure points. About 1,300 people were helped from there [S32]. Stræde: ten "velansete lokale borgere" (respected local citizens), the "Jødekomiteen", none of them in the resistance, organized passage for 1,300 from the harbour and nearby beaches [S13].
- 6 October 1943: the crowds were so great that the Danish coast police closed the harbour area. Refugees hid "i menighedshuset og på loftet af Gilleleje kirke" (in the parish hall and the church loft) [S11].
- "Da Gestapo ankom senere på aftenen, blev over 100 jøder arresteret" (when the Gestapo arrived later that evening, over 100 Jews were arrested). That count covers the parish hall and the loft together [S11].
- **Date:** "the night between October 6 and 7" (VisitNorthZealand) [S30]; "the night between the 6th and 7 October 1943" (Wikipedia, citing Lidegaard) [S33]; 6 October (Bak) [S11][S8]. TV2 Kosmopol instead writes "natten til den 6. oktober" [S29]. That is an outlier.
- **Number in the loft (sources differ):** 60 (TV2, citing Søren Frandsen's 2023 Museum Nordsjælland book; VisitNorthZealand says "around 60" and also "more than 60") [S29][S30]; 80 (VisitDenmark church listing) [S31]; 85 (Wikipedia, citing Lidegaard's *Landsmænd*, 2013) [S33]. TV2 gives 125 Jews taken in Gilleleje over the whole of October 1943, 60 of them in the loft [S29].
- **Who and how:** the Gestapo, led by Hans Juhl of Helsingør [S29]. Bak (2009) says an informer betrayed them [S8]. VisitDenmark says they were "denounced" [S31]. Frandsen's book reports an informer among Gilleleje's fishermen, in a wider context [S29].
- **Deportation:** Bak (2009): 50 were deported after the Gilleleje betrayal [S8], and her folkedrab article also gives "ca. 50" deported [S11]. So more were arrested than deported. VisitDenmark's "all ... brought to Theresienstadt" [S31] conflicts with Bak and should not be used. Most of those taken from the loft came back after the war [S30].
- Tourism-page detail, not corroborated elsewhere: a young man hid in the church tower, was taken the next day, escaped in Copenhagen and drowned trying to flee [S30].

## 7. Numbers who reached Sweden

- USHMM: "some 7,200 Jews and 700 of their non-Jewish relatives" [S1][S3]. In another place USHMM gives "some 7,200 Danish Jews and 680 non-Jewish family members", and its map caption says "more than 7,000 Jews and close to 700" [S2].
- Bak: "I alt 7056 jøder" fled in October 1943, among them 1,301 "half-Jews" and 1,236 children under 18. With them came 686 non-Jewish spouses: "næsten 8000 mennesker" (almost 8,000 people) [S11]. Bak 2009 says 8,000, about 95% of the community [S8].
- Kirchhoff: "flere end 7000" (more than 7,000), in 600–700 transports from the north and east coasts of Zealand [S22].
- Stræde mentions 7,600 counted in Sweden [S13]. He gives at least 30,000 to 40,000 helpers [S13].
- The Swedish refugee protocols (over 6,000 reports covering routes, arrival times and payment terms) are online in the Danish Jewish Museum's Safe Haven database. That is a primary source for checking routes and prices [S7].

## 8. Theresienstadt

- **How many (sources differ):**
  - 470 (USHMM; folkedrab English) [S1][S2][S17];
  - 472 (Bak) [S11][S8][S18];
  - 481 (Kirchhoff, Lex) [S22];
  - "fewer than 500" (Kirchhoff 2013) [S23].
- USHMM breaks the 470 down as 456 in October 1943 on three transports plus 14 in 1944 [S4]. Folkedrab gives the transports:
  - Aalborg, 83 prisoners, arrived 5 October;
  - Copenhagen by ship, 198 prisoners, arrived 6 October;
  - Horserød, 175 prisoners, arrived 14 October;
  - Horserød on 23 November, 16 prisoners to Sachsenhausen/Ravensbrück, 14 of whom reached Theresienstadt in 1944 [S17].
- About a third were stateless. The oldest was 89. Forty-eight were children aged 0–15 [S17].
- **Danish pressure:** "The Danish authorities and the Danish Red Cross vocally and insistently demanded information" [S1]. Bak (2009): an Eichmann–Best agreement kept Danish Jews from being deported further east [S8]. USHMM suggests Danish protests may have prevented deportation to killing centres, and says survival was largely due to Danish officials' pressure [S2].
- **Parcels:** in December 1943 Danish authorities obtained an agreement that each deportee could receive a clothing parcel from Denmark. Copenhagen's Socialtjenesten collected the deportees' own clothes [S14]. Food parcels arrived from spring 1944, at first sent by private people through the post. Official permission came in summer 1944, roughly once a month. The Social Ministry paid for most, through the "Fund of 1944 for Social and Humanitarian Purposes", and "the Red Cross took over some of the deliveries" [S18]. Letters and monthly parcels were also allowed for German Jews deported from Denmark [S8].
- **The visit of 23 June 1944:** the visitors were Maurice Rossel (ICRC), Eigil Juel Henningsen (Danish Health Authority, for the Danish Red Cross) and Frants Hvass (Danish Foreign Ministry). The visit came about through repeated Danish requests [S4].
  - Before it: the camp was "beautified", and about 7,500 Jews were deported to Auschwitz on 15–18 May 1944 to reduce crowding [S4][S19]. On 22 June the Danish prisoners were moved into better housing and coached [S4].
  - Afterwards: all three visitors wrote positive reports, and Hvass told Best the delegation had been impressed [S4]. The Danish representatives later admitted they had seen that much was staged but kept quiet to protect the prisoners [S19].
- **Deaths (sources differ slightly):**
  - "51 adults and two babies born in the camp", about 10% of the Danish prisoners (folkedrab) [S21];
  - 51 died in the camp, plus two infants born there (Bak) [S11];
  - "all but 51 survived" (USHMM) [S2];
  - 52 (Kirchhoff, Lex; Bak 2009) [S22][S8];
  - 50 (Kirchhoff 2013) [S23].
- Two more deportees died elsewhere: one was sent on from Theresienstadt to a death camp, and one went with the fourth transport to Sachsenhausen and then Majdanek [S21]. Counting deaths during the flight as well, "some 120 Danish Jews died" [S1].
- **White Buses:** in February 1945 Count Folke Bernadotte negotiated with Himmler. Lex credits the Danish initiative to the civil servants H. H. Koch (Social Ministry) and Frants Hvass, says the practical execution was largely Danish, and lists 423 Danish Jews from Theresienstadt among those brought out [S24][S20].
  - 13 April 1945: the Danish prisoners were told they were going home [S20].
  - After waiting a day and a half they boarded, and 423 people were released [S20]. USHMM dates this to 15 April: "the SS permitted Swedish Red Cross personnel to rescue 423 Jews, most of whom were from Denmark" [S5].
  - 17 April: the buses reached the Danish border. They stopped overnight in Odense, then went to Copenhagen, and the group sailed to Sweden for quarantine. People came home after liberation [S20].

## 9. Return in 1945: both sides

- The first ferry of refugees from Sweden reached Copenhagen's Frihavn in late May 1945. Until 10 June about 1,200 people arrived each day [S15]. "Virtually all of the refugees returned to Denmark in 1945" [S1].
- **Looked after:** some families found home and belongings "intakte og velplejede" (intact and well kept). "Et stort antal private danskere" (a large number of private Danes) had looked after the families' property "uegennyttigt og omhyggeligt" (unselfishly and carefully) [S15].
- **Copenhagen's Socialtjenesten** (municipal): it inspected empty homes, listed contents, cleaned and locked them, and paid rent where the lease could be kept. Otherwise it crated and stored furniture, and it made custody agreements with neighbours, relatives and employees [S14]. Up to April 1945 it carried out 1,970 investigations, paid rent and costs for 97 flats and stored 350 households' goods. The 1945 cost was 150,000 kr., refunded by the Social Ministry [S14].
- **Lost:** others found belongings "solgt eller stjålet" (sold or stolen), and homes left unprotected "i over 19 måneder" (for over 19 months). Some flats had been "overdraget til andre" (handed to others), some powers of attorney were abused, and there was "egentlig misbrug og tyveri" (real abuse and theft). Businesses were "misligholdt eller gået fallit" (neglected or bankrupt) [S15].
- "Mindst 65%" (at least 65%) of Danish Jews needed and received financial help on return; 4,200 received temporary aid [S15]. About one in five returning refugees spent time in temporary housing, where conditions were "kaotiske og nervepirrende" [S15].
- Under the compensation law of 1 October 1945 roughly 2.2 million kr. was paid out. It applied only to Danish citizens, so stateless refugees were excluded, and payments had a ceiling [S15]. Many lost businesses and savings or took on debt [S28].

## 10. Denmark's German minority after 1945 (keep separate from the refugees)

**The established minority in South Jutland (North Schleswig)**
- The minority has lived in Denmark since 1920, when North Schleswig was reunited with Denmark after the referendums. After the war the reorganized minority declared its loyalty to Denmark and recognized the 1920 border. The 1955 Bonn–Copenhagen Declarations set out minority rights on both sides [S37]. Today it numbers about 10,000–15,000 [S37]. Thaler gives about 30,000 North Schleswigers in 1945 [S38].
- The response was judicial, not expulsion: about 3,500 arrested and nearly 3,000 sentenced, mostly for service in German military and paramilitary units. Thaler contrasts this with the summary deportation of Germans from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, and says the minority was able to recover [S38].
- About 3,500 German-minded Danes were interned in Fårhuslejren (formerly the Frøslev camp) from 1945 to 1949, and about 3,000 were sentenced [S39][S40].
- Overall, 13,500 Danes were convicted in the retsopgør (legal purge), half of them for German war service. "Næsten hver fjerde" (almost one in four) came from the German minority (Henrik Skov Kristensen) [S40][S39]. Lex says "over 13.000" were convicted of treason [S46].
- Kristensen judges the purge of the minority "rimeligt" (reasonable) for its time. The minority saw, and may still see, Fårhus and the purge as "dybt uretfærdigt" (deeply unjust) [S39].

**German refugees of 1945 (a different group)**
- About 250,000 German refugees, about 5% of the population, were in Denmark in 1945–49 [S42][S41]. Arrival counts differ: "over 200.000" [S44], "over 230.000" refugees at liberation, about 34,000 of them non-German [S45], and about 350,000 arriving in total by 4 May 1945 (Jensen, Oksbøl) [S43].
- They were held in camps "bag pigtrådshegn" (behind barbed-wire fences) and could not mix with Danes [S42]. Oksbøl was fenced and guarded, and contact with Danes was forbidden. At its peak in 1946 it held over 35,000 people [S43][S44].
- Repatriation began in November 1946. The last transport left on 15 February 1949 [S45][S42].
- These were refugees from Germany's eastern provinces [S41], not members of the Danish German minority. Don't mix the two.

## Unverified / do not state as fact

- That the warning was given at a *morning* service, or the exact words Melchior used. Only TIME (cancelled service, "hide or flee") and Rühl/Bak ("New Year service a few days before") were read. No fetched source names Krystalgade.
- Bispebjerg figures such as "1,500–2,000 hidden" or "2,000 helped". These appear only on blogs and advocacy sites. Yad Vashem and the USHMM collection record for Ole Secher could not be loaded.
- That Gilleleje was betrayed by "a Danish girl in love with a German soldier". This appeared only in search snippets from Wikipedia-derived pages, which were not read in full. Bak says only "an informer" [S8], and Museum Nordsjælland's researcher points to an informer among the fishermen [S29].
- Any single "typical" price, and the claim that prices fell *within* October (850 → 1,500 → 500 kr.). That comes from an unverified forum post citing Swedish police reports. Claims of 100,000 kr. fares are also unconfirmed.
- That *all* the Gilleleje loft prisoners were deported (VisitDenmark). This conflicts with Bak.
- The C. B. Henriques chain of warning, and Duckwitz's private meeting details. These come from secondary sources and Duckwitz's own account. Kirchhoff notes that historians are "still operating within Duckwitz's own narrative" [S23].
- The King Christian X yellow-star story. Bak calls it "det pure opspind" (pure invention) [S16]. Never use it.

## Open questions / uncertain

- Night of 1–2 October: 232 (German sources, Rühl) or 202 in Copenhagen (Bak, citing Kreth & Mogensen). Forces: 1,500, more than 1,800, or 1,500 Orpo plus 3,000 Sipo.
- Total deported: 470, 472 or 481. Deaths: 50, 51, 52, or 51 plus 2 babies.
- Gilleleje: 60, 80 or 85 in the loft, over 100 arrested in total, about 50 deported. Why so many fewer were deported than arrested is not explained in the fetched sources.
- Whether the Wehrmacht was ordered to assist (Bak in folkedrab, Kirchhoff) or ordered not to interfere (Bak 2009).
- Children placed in foster care: about 130 or at least 150.
